# Folk and Cultures in Art — the cast, and the traps

Read for any image containing a folk, a culture or a named person. The period and material
rules that sit under this are in [art-setting.md](art-setting.md); the palette lock and modesty
rules in [art-direction.md](art-direction.md) outrank everything here.

## Folk belong in the banners too, and they read by silhouette

**An unqualified figure comes back human.** The first banner pass named a folk in one plate out
of 43 and the model defaulted every other one to humans — a setting with twelve folk illustrated
as a human world with a folk chapter attached. Every scene with a figure in it names who those
figures are. Three rules make that affordable:

- **Cast from the Eternal Desert.** The game is set in the Great Desert, so the banner cast is
  its cultures (the table below). Reaching across the world for whichever culture best fits a
  scene is how a Mahruk quay, an Orgon armoury and a Morvael treasury got into a desert library.
  Folk with no desert culture appear only where all twelve must show. **Name the culture, not the
  folk** — "a dwarf" drifts to generic fantasy, the full Ghahar clause does not. Keep one
  appearance per folk across the whole library, from the single cast table.
- **Anchor the plate to one host culture whose niche supplies the scene**, and let the culture
  change with the subject rather than mixing everywhere: the materials hall, the aqueduct and the
  mason's yard are Ghahar, the delta is Vaashk, the observatory is Hataar, the court is Anutep,
  the labour is Khatok. A mix in every plate breaks the density rule and reads as a parade.
  Reserve the full mix for pages whose subject genuinely *is* variety — folk, languages,
  backgrounds, skills, talents, a bazaar, a caravan, a training ground.
- **At banner scale a face carries nothing, so folk identity is silhouette work**: height, build,
  horns, upright ears, tails, manes, hooves, the cut of a beard. Name those, not features. A hune
  standing beside a gnome also does the register's scale contrast for free.

Carry the shared qualifier in a **fixed block, not per scene** — "clearly humanoid people rather
than animals, in the same kilts and mantles as everyone else", plus a clause holding fur, scale
and skin inside the palette. Forty near-copies of that sentence drift; one block does not. Left
unstated, a five-folk crowd arrives in five new hues and blows the six-colour cap.

In a crowd or a battle, the folk name goes inside **every** crowd clause and sides are assigned
by geography — see [art-composition.md](art-composition.md) § Sides are assigned by geography.

**Some cultures on that list are villains, and a niche match is not enough to cast one.** The
**Urduk** run a slave-mining dictatorship and were handed the weapon-and-armour-materials plate
purely because they smelt bronze — which reads as a rules page endorsing them. They belong only
where the scene *means* oppression, curse or threat, which is also where their iron belongs.
**Zakhar** orcs are raiders with no settlements, so they fit a raid or a confrontation, not a
workshop. When an honest-craft scene needs dwarves it is **Ghahar**: canon gives them the stone,
the hydraulics, the bronze, and the roller-spider chitin worked by dedicated chitin-former
guilds, which covers armour material better than a furnace does.

**"Kesheret" is a place, not a people.** It is the Red Land — dune seas, canyons, tar pits and
rock savanna (`(03) 🗺️ Geographie/Regionen/Kesheret.md`). "A Kesheret artificer" is a category
error that put a whole plate in the wrong subject: the sorcerer-princes are a **political
entity** ruling a **culturally Sefkari** population, so their figure clause is a Sefkari one with
a robe and veil over it. The **Kerbei** also live in Kesheret, in the ruins, and are a different
people entirely.

Two folk have **no** Great Desert culture and so appear only as visitors, or in the folk plate
that must show all twelve: **satyrs** (island vineyard and grove folk) and **catfolk other than
the Jakharii**. Two desert-adjacent cultures are traps rather than options — **Gajahar**
"elephant hune" have no anatomy in the vault beyond the baseline hune, so the name returns an
elephant, and **Jinkai** are defined by their steel, which collides with the iron taboo.

## A bare garment noun returns the modern default, and it beats the fixed blocks

"A heavy-tailed Vaashk lizardfolk asleep against a column **with a hat over their face**"
produced a cowboy hat. *Hat* has no period attached, so the model supplies the one it knows, and
a scene clause is specific enough to override the general costume line in the folk block. The
materials clause in the palette block did not catch it either: it enumerates "tool, weapon,
fitting or vessel" and has never covered **clothing**.

- **Never write a garment noun without its material and construction.** Not "a hat" but "a broad
  shade woven from palm leaf"; not "a cloak" but "a matting cloak". Same rule as "name garments,
  never periods" in [art-registers.md](art-registers.md) § Register B, but subtler — no wrong
  period was named, nothing was named at all, and the blank got filled.
- **The folk block carries a conditional headwear clause**, phrased existentially so it does not
  furnish bare-headed scenes with hats: "*wherever* a head covering *happens to appear*, it is a
  length of cloth wound flat around the head and down over the neck, a draped shawl, or a broad
  shade woven from reed or palm leaf." Headwear is not a per-culture trait, so it belongs in a
  fixed block — unlike the beard, which does not.

## A species noun carries a pop-culture default that outranks anything you write about it

Hit twice, on the two most heavily loaded names: **dwarf** returns a braided beard, **gnome** a
red pointed cap. In both cases the description was present and correct and simply lost to the
noun. Assume the loaded default is the model's first answer and prompt against it directly:

- **Never let the species noun stand alone.** Bind the anatomy to it in the same phrase, every
  time it appears, including on background figures. "A small furred Burrin gnome" produced a
  garden gnome across eleven banners; it now reads "a small sandy-pelted Burrin gnome with
  outsized ears, a bare human face and a length of undyed sand-coloured cloth wound flat around
  the head and down over the neck."
- **Name the head covering as a positive shape, and never call it a hood** when the folk's
  default is a pointed hat. "Layered hooded desert robe" is one short step from a cap; **wound
  flat around the head** cannot become a point.
- **State the cloth colour** so the palette block's free accent has nowhere to land — see
  [art-direction.md](art-direction.md) § Palette lock.

The Auflin get the same treatment as the Burrin, being gnomes too.

### The two dwarf cultures are told apart by the beard

Ghahar wear the **Babylonian** beard, Urduk the **Assyrian** one. "Braided" was the library's
default and is wrong for either: it gave the Ghahar a generic fantasy-dwarf plait — exactly the
drift the cast table exists to stop — and erased the only cheap way to tell the two apart. The
shapes differ in **outline**, which is what makes them usable at banner scale: the Ghahar beard
is a **tapering vertical mass, blunt at the bottom**; the Urduk beard is a **squared block in
horizontal tiers**. Height, build and skin will not separate two four-foot dwarf peoples; the
beard will.

**Name the real-world reference in the prompt — the desert's own anchor is not what that rule
forbids.** The eastern-mist-realms rule ("say the region, not the real culture", in
[art-setting.md](art-setting.md)) was wrongly applied to the beards, so the prompts carried pure
geometry and the renders came back with generic fantasy plaits anyway. The two cases are
opposite: that rule protects the desert from a **foreign** period leaking in, and Mesopotamia is
not foreign here — it is the setting's own anchor — so naming a Mesopotamian monument imports
precisely what is wanted, in one phrase.

- Ghahar: *"dressed in the ancient Mesopotamian manner of the stele of Hammurabi and the Akkadian
  kings"*, then **one solid squared mass, level hem cut straight across, the surface grooved into
  narrow parallel vertical channels like the fluting of a stone column**.
- Urduk: *"dressed in the manner of Neo-Assyrian palace relief"*, then **squared hem, stacked
  horizontal rows of tight spiral ringlets alternating with bands of crimped waves**.

Keep the geometry after the reference rather than instead of it: the reference sets the family,
the geometry picks the member. The geometry-only attempt had two failure modes worth recognising
anywhere a specific historical form is asked for — it **described texture but never the shape of
the mass**, which is what a model anchors on, and it used a self-cancelling pair ("straight
vertical crimped strands" is both straight and not straight). **Give the silhouette first, the
surface second.**

**Reserve the named monument for Register B.** A folk portrait shows the beard at full size and
can afford the phrase; a banner has twenty other things in it and "stele" near a figure invites
the model to carve one. Banner clauses carry the short shape form only — "long square-cut
Mesopotamian relief beards, grooved in narrow vertical flutes".

## Skin

**Never name a metal as a skin colour.** The vault calls Ghahar skin "gold-bronze", and carrying
that word into a prompt made the model render *metal* — polished, specular, exactly the glossy
highlight the brief forbids, and worst on the armor plate where real bronze fills the frame. Name
the colour, not the material: "dark skin of a deep golden brown, **matt and dusty**". Same care
for the hune, whose skin is stone-grey — say the grey and the weathering, never a polished stone.

The **Anutep are dark-skinned**, as are the Sefkari and Khatok. The vault does not state it and
the model will not assume it, so every sun-elf clause carries it: the desert's ruling culture is
not a pale one. Imentep pallor is the *exception that proves it* — the same dark skin faded
ash-grey by generations underground, which is why it reads as a curse rather than as a different
stock.

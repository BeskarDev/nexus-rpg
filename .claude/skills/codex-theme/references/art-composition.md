# Plate Composition — building a Register A scene

Register A only. Style spec: [art-registers.md](art-registers.md) § Register A. Who is *in* the
scene: [art-folk.md](art-folk.md), [art-gods.md](art-gods.md),
[art-creatures.md](art-creatures.md). Every rule below was learned from a plate coming back
wrong; the quoted phrases are reusable prompt text.

## Density — fix it in the fixed blocks, not per scene

Left alone the model renders every figure individually, gives each its own cloth colour and
fills the frame edge to edge: a detailed digital illustration that turns to mush at banner size.
Three levers:

- **Cap the colour count and say clothing is included** — otherwise a twenty-figure crowd
  arrives in twenty hues.
- **Name what gets simplified**: "suggest carved script and brickwork with a handful of marks";
  "anyone below middle distance is a blank silhouette".
- **State the display size and the element budget**: "printed small … three or four things the
  eye can land on, sitting in open space". This beats any adjective about style, because it gives
  the model a reason.

Per scene, name what *carries* detail ("only the priest and the tablet carry any detail") rather
than listing more things to draw.

## The enormous element must be the page's topic, and it must be acting

An inert enormous element leaves the plate with no subject, so the model elects one from the
crowd — an awning spanning the equipment banner handed focus to a goblin.

- **It must be the topic and it must be doing something**, even passively: a beam tipping under a
  load, a furnace pouring, a statue taking arrows. Architecture that merely spans the frame
  cannot hold focus.
- **A figure given a narrative beat becomes the subject, whatever its size.** "A goblin already
  walking off with something" is a story and beats four figures holding postures. Keep background
  figures to plain working postures; one named figure carries the beat.
- Distinctive silhouettes compound this — a large-eared goblin or a horned minotaur outdraws a
  human of the same size — so say outright what carries detail rather than hoping scale sorts it.

## It must also be plausibly monumental

Architecture, statuary, relief, a landform, a beast, a stele, a stockpile. **Never an object
whose size is part of what it is**: "an enormous ceremonial bronze sickle-sword, taller than the
building behind it" comes back a comic prop, and a weapons page that cannot show a believable
weapon has failed at its one job. The equipment plate hit this twice — a giant awning (inert),
then a giant balance.

**When the object itself must be the subject, isolate it and converge attention on it — never
enlarge it, and never bring the camera in.** A close foreground hand holding the piece up was
rejected: **the register's camera is always an observer at a distance**, and one POV plate among
forty wide ones reads as a different book. The emphasis kit, all four together:

- **Put it alone in open ground** — one small object on a low table in a bare swept court is
  unmissable precisely because nothing else is there.
- **One hard-edged patch of sunlight** across the paving with the object in it. A flat cast-light
  shape is not rendering.
- **Point every figure at it**, held back at the frame's edges, every head turned.
- **Name it in the detail clause** and nothing else.

Otherwise, when the topic *is* a hand object, monumentalise a **depiction** of it and let the real
ones sit at true size beside it: a colossal relief of a god with a sickle-sword raised, "every
weapon in the yard below is ordinary hand size against it". A **stockpile** also works, a pile
being legitimately huge — sacks, bales, jars and crates stacked over the rooftops.

## Attention is not interaction — pay for isolation with a beat pointing inward

"Everyone stands well back and every one of them is looking at it" does *not* satisfy the
acceptance rule that subjects interact. It returns a **standoff**: two mirrored rows across an
empty lit square, arms down, nobody touching anything, the composition split down the centre
line. Focus achieved, scene dead.

The beat-steals-the-subject rule was over-applied into giving no figure a beat at all. The
distinction is **direction, not presence** — the goblin and the gnome stole their plates because
their beats pointed *away*. A beat aimed **at** the object stacks with the isolation.

- **One figure acts on the object, low and close to it** — kneeling to offer it up across both
  open palms, drawing the cloth back off it, lifting it from its stand.
- **One figure receives, bent in over it**, a hand out and not yet touching, the two heads nearly
  meeting above it. The gap between hand and object is the tension, and it sits where the eye
  already goes.
- **Stagger everyone else off the centre line.** Two groups meeting mid-frame arrange themselves
  as facing ranks; bring one party in from a corner **on a diagonal**, halted in a loose group
  with **none of them level with each other**.
- **Give the arrival its occasion** — raised trumpets, a banner on a pole, porters still coming
  down the plank, a carried sunshade, a scribe crouched over a tablet recording the deal. These
  read as event rather than queue, and each is a small held action, so none can be aimed at the
  wrong target.

## A fight needs one contact point, and the enormous element must not sit in the gap

A toppled column across the whole strip with raiders on one side and militia on the other is not
a battle, it is two crowds separated by a log — the standoff in armour. Four rules that compound:

- **Never put the enormous element between the factions.** A mass spanning the frame becomes a
  wall, and a wall guarantees the sides never touch. Run it *along* the action, put it behind
  them, or make it the thing being crossed **at a single breach** so contact happens on it.
- **The named beat must be cross-faction.** "The two orcs hauling each other up" is
  *intra*-faction, so the story became orcs helping each other climb and the plate argued against
  its own page. Name two enemies locked on one object: a spear shaft caught on a shield rim, both
  pairs of hands on it.
- **One contact point, and say so.** Three groups in three places gives three unrelated things.
  Say "everything that matters happens at that one point", place it off-centre, and give every
  other figure a plain braced or crouching posture where they already stand.
- **Factions read as two crowd SHAPES, not as individuals.** At banner size neither a face nor a
  costume detail carries, so "tusked orcs" against "tattooed humans" will not separate two
  crowds. Give the masses different *outlines* — "a heavy compact clump bunched at the crossing"
  against "a thin scattered row spread along the stone and thinning away to the left".

**Keep the smoke off the contact point** — atmosphere goes where the fight is not.

### Sides are assigned by geography and by repeating the folk name

- **Name the folk inside every crowd clause**, not only on the figures in the beat. "Broad tusked
  figures in bronze scale harness" attaches no species, and *an unqualified figure comes back
  human* applies to crowds as much as to lone figures — both armies rendered human. The
  repetition is what binds the label to the mass.
- **A contact point is a location and gives no direction.** Assign each faction a **side of the
  dividing element and a facing** (everyone beyond the stone and higher is one army facing down
  and left; everyone in front and lower is the other, facing up and right), then say outright
  that the dividing element **is** the front line and that which side a figure stands on tells you
  whose side they are on. Geography does what costume cannot at this size.
- **State exclusivity positively**: "every single figure beyond the stone is a Zakhar orc",
  "every fight in this picture is one orc against one defender". Forbidding the mixture names it.
- **A mixed defending force against a uniform attacking one is a help** — one army all alike, the
  other visibly composite, is itself a faction cue. The combat plate has Khatok militia stiffened
  by two taller Anutep sun-elf officers. Give the elite figures a role pointing at the
  contact point (one in the knot with both hands on the enemy's spear shaft, one further back with
  an arm thrown toward it) so the extra folk buys legibility rather than a third vignette.

## On a mechanics page, draw the mechanic, not the fiction around it

A burst canal embankment with a work party fighting the breach is a plate about *labour*, and
Challenges are a progress clock: a die at maximum, one step off per success, a consequence on
every failure. **Ask what the rule measures, then find the composition that shows the
measurement.**

Distance still to run is stages, so the plate stages: the quarry mid-leap close and large, the
first pursuer a stride behind, the second snagged and smaller, two more small and pale on the
skyline. Each plane further back is a step not yet taken, and atmospheric perspective is already
the grammar for it. **Draw a failure into the frame too** — the snagged pursuer *is* the
consequence entry from the table.

This buys one licensed exception to "atmosphere, not incident": **a single arrested instant is not
a frame full of action.** The enormous element stays architecture, the sky keeps its half, only
two figures carry detail, and what makes it a chase rather than a journey is that one figure is in
the air.

### Haste has to be built, because the register's default mood is still

Urgency must be asked for explicitly, and never with faces — an expression is invisible at 4:1.
Four things that work, all silhouette or wreckage:

- **Put every body off vertical, as a blanket clause**: "every figure in the picture is off
  vertical and not one of them has both feet on a roof" reaches the background figures the scene
  never described individually.
- **Leave something still in the air** — objects spilling from a pouch, cloth torn loose and
  streaming, birds bursting up. A falling thing cannot read as stationary, which a running figure
  alone always risks.
- **Show the wreckage of the stage already passed** — a plank kicked askew and hanging off the
  edge says the route was taken at speed without drawing a second figure.
- **A backward glance over the shoulder** — the cheapest urgency read there is, and it survives
  being a silhouette.

**Make the route change level and direction**, reading as one continuous broken line the eye
follows from a named corner to a named exit. A straight recession is a road; stairs, a
doubled-back parapet, a plank and then a gap is a *scramble*, which also gives the staged-progress
read something to stage along.

Motion cues are texture, not focal elements, so they do not spend the three-or-four budget — but
keep them to two or three or they become the subject.

### Tension is not motion, and the mechanic often has a period instrument

Social Intrigue tracks **Patience**, a die ticking down on every roll, so its plate is an audience
where the clock and not the argument decides. That is held breath, and it inverts every haste
lever: **everything in the frame is still except one thing, and the one moving thing is the
clock.** A single moving element in a frozen court is more tense than a frame of action, because
it is the only thing to watch and it is running out.

- **Look for the period instrument that *is* the mechanic.** Patience became a monumental stone
  **outflow water clock**, close to empty, water falling in one thin bright line — the Egyptian
  clepsydra is attested at Karnak around 1400 BCE, so it needs no licence. It also solves the
  enormous-element problem: an instrument is plausibly monumental *and* is doing something.
- **Point the decider at the instrument, not at the speaker.** The arbiter's head turned away
  from the petitioner and across the court toward the falling water says "patience is deciding
  this" in one silhouette, and a crowd of heads all turned the same way, none speaking, does the
  rest — turned heads carry at 4:1 where expressions carry nothing.

Keep the instrument off the axis between the two parties, and say the floor between them is
**swept clear** — the emptiness makes the two sides read as facing each other rather than as one
crowd.

## A full-width horizontal mass turns the horizon into a panel border

A gate "beneath a huge open sky" across the whole strip makes its top edge a hard horizontal cut:
sky in the upper band, scene in the lower, two stacked comic panels rather than one place. Any
full-width horizontal mass does this — a gate, a courtyard wall, a relief wall, a cliff face. Use
all three fixes:

- **Run the structure off the top edge** rather than letting it terminate inside the frame. An
  edge that leaves the canvas cannot divide it.
- **Leave one side open to ground level.** Put the mass in the left or right two thirds and let
  the sky come *all the way down* to the ground on the other side, so the zones interlock instead
  of stacking.
- **Put something across the join** — smoke or dust drifting up over the face, a long diagonal
  shadow thrown into the yard, birds, a hanging awning, a crane arm.

State this in the scene, not the fixed blocks, since it depends where the mass sits. The
atmospheric recession also needs somewhere to happen, and a wall filling the middle distance
leaves it nowhere.

## Underground: substitute a graded cavern roof for the sky, never just remove it

The sky clause is really an **upper-half-of-graded-air** clause, buying vastness up top plus the
one licensed place for tonal modelling, which is what holds the value range mid-weight for both
site themes. Interior plates usually honour it cheaply with sky through a collapsed vault, but
that will not serve a scene whose subject *is* being underground: the **Imentep** shy from
daylight, so a sunbeam in their sanctum contradicts the people in it.

Fill the upper 40–60% with **an enormous cavern roof — a great pale dome of stone, softly graded
and gently modelled exactly as a sky would be**, hung with long formations and receding into
paler, cooler air toward the back. Atmospheric perspective works on cave air too. Two things must
be said outright or the plate goes black:

- **Name the stone pale and cap the value range** — "the whole cavern is pale limestone and the
  darkest values in the picture stay mid-weight". Left unstated, "underground" returns near-black,
  which blows out against one of the two themes.
- **Give the light a warm source and say it is the only other light.** Phosphor-stone lamps
  burning **dull warm amber** keep the 80% warm core intact and leave rune-cyan as the only cool
  colour in frame, which it must be on an arcane plate.

## A party reads as a party by being countable, and emptiness is stated positively

A caravan strung along a cliff of switchbacks with laden camels comes back as freight logistics.
Travel here is a **small party with assigned roles**, and a fellowship is defined by a number.

- **Give the count and make it countable** — "five people and one animal, few enough to count at a
  glance". Plural nouns with no number scale up on their own, and one pack animal is the strongest
  tell that this is a party rather than a trade train. Cluster the group into one knot as a single
  focal element and put one figure — the Scout — far out and higher, alone, as the second.
- **Roles read as one action each, and the canon roles have canon folk**: Hataar hune are pass
  astronomers and caravan pilots, so a hune sighting a slate star-chart at the landmark *is* the
  Navigator; Burrin gnomes are water-finders and guides; Jakharii are savanna hunters, so the
  Jakharii alone on the rock *is* the Scout. Pick the folk whose canon niche is the role and it
  needs no explaining.
- **State emptiness as the one mark that is there, never as an absence.** "There is no road and no
  other travellers" names a road and a crowd, and by the negation rule that is how they get drawn.
  Use a positive substitute that does more: "the only mark anywhere on the land is the single line
  of their own footprints, coming up over the crest behind them; every other slope is unbroken
  sand." The trail is also the days already walked, so it pays for the progress clock too.
- **A landmark is a checkpoint in the rules, so make the enormous element one** — a toppled
  half-buried colossus with sand streaming off its brow is monumental, is doing something, and is
  what the Navigator sights on. Carry the distance with three recession planes to a mountain line,
  not by staging the party along a route; **that device belongs to `challenges-banner`**, and
  travel differentiates on the scale of the land instead.
- **The Bronze Age pack animal is the donkey.** The Arabian camel is not domesticated inside the
  setting's window, and the donkey reads smaller and humbler, which helps the plate say "party"
  rather than "caravan".

## "Tiny" is read absolutely, so never attach it to a folk with a stated height

"Tiny horned Enkeddu minotaur porters … seven feet tall" produced minotaurs *smaller* than every
other humanoid in frame. "Tiny by comparison" is shorthand for a relationship, but the model glues
the adjective to its noun and shrinks the folk itself.

- Use "tiny" only for unnamed background figures, or for folk that are genuinely small.
- Otherwise express smallness as a **ratio against the enormous element** — "the jars are three
  times the height of a tall person … even so their horns only reach the jar's shoulder".
- In a mixed group state the **internal height order**: "the largest people in the granary", "a
  head shorter than they are", "no higher than the elf's waist". A folk set is read comparatively,
  and a banner that inverts the order teaches the reader the wrong thing.
